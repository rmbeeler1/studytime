    Pre-mRNA Basics

    All RNA is transcribed from a DNA template. The flow of genetic information is linear, in one direction: DNA is transcribed into RNA, and messenger RNA (mRNA) is translated into protein.

    Why eukaryotic RNA gets processed

    In eukaryotes, transcription and translation are separated in time and space. Transcription occurs in the nucleus; translation occurs in the cytoplasm. That gap is an opportunity for the RNA transcript to be modified before it is translated.

    • Precursor mRNA (pre-mRNA): the initial, direct transcription product of a DNA template, inside the nucleus.
    • Messenger RNA (mRNA): processed, mature mRNA, moved to the cytoplasm for translation.

    Mature mRNA structure

    • Untranslated regions (UTRs): parts of the mRNA that are not translated into protein.
    • Protein-coding region: the sequence that is translated into protein, flanked by the 5′ and 3′ untranslated regions. It begins at the start codon and ends at the stop codon.

    In order (Fig. 14.16): the 5′ cap is added soon after transcription initiation, the 3′ end is cleaved and polyadenylated, and the introns are spliced.

    Capping

    The 5′ cap is added soon after transcription initiation. Recall that nucleotides are joined by phosphodiester bonds in an alternating phosphate-ribose backbone (5′ to 3′), and that the first nucleotide of a pre-mRNA has three phosphates.

    Adding the cap (Fig. 14.6)

    1. One of the three phosphate groups at the 5′ end is removed.
    2. A guanine nucleotide (with its phosphate group) is added. It comes from GTP, releasing PPi.
    3. Methyl groups are added to position 7 of the base of the terminal guanine, making 7-methylguanosine.
    4. Methyl groups are added to the 2′ position of the sugar in the second and sometimes third nucleotides.
    5. If the base on the second nucleotide is A, that base may also be methylated.

    The cap guanine is joined to the mRNA by a unique 5′-5′ bond.

    What the cap does

    • The cap is bound by the Cap Binding Complex (CBC).
    • Cap + CBC facilitate binding of the ribosome to the 5′ end of mRNA, so the cap promotes translation (clearer in Chapter 15).
    • The cap increases mRNA stability.
    • Cap + CBC block 5′ to 3′ exonucleases.

    Link to Chapter 13: Rat1

    In RNA polymerase II termination (Fig. 13.19), the RNA is cleaved to generate its 3′ end, and Rat1, a 5′ to 3′ exonuclease, attaches to the 5′ end of the trailing RNA and degrades it. Adding CBC blocks Rat1 exonuclease activity on the pre-mRNA, which prevents degradation of the mRNA that was just made.

    Polyadenylation

    The poly(A) tail is 50 to 250 adenine nucleotides added at the 3′ end. These adenines are not encoded in the DNA; they are added after transcription.

    How the tail is added (Fig. 14.7)

    1. The pre-mRNA has a consensus sequence, AAUAAA, and a U-rich sequence further downstream.
    2. The pre-mRNA is cleaved 11 to 30 nucleotides downstream of the consensus sequence, in the 3′ untranslated region.
    3. Adenine nucleotides are added to the new 3′ end (polyadenylation), generating the poly(A) tail.
    4. The tail is bound by poly(A) binding proteins (PBPs).
    ConclusionIn pre-mRNA processing, a poly(A) tail is added through cleavage and polyadenylation. The tail increases mRNA stability and facilitates binding of the ribosome to mRNA.

    Introns and Exons

    Colinearity

    Many prokaryotic genes are colinear: a continuous sequence of nucleotides in the DNA encodes a continuous sequence of amino acids in the protein, so the number of nucleotides in the gene is proportional to the number of amino acids in the protein (Fig. 14.1).

    Most eukaryotic genes are not colinear. In the experiment of Fig. 14.2, DNA was mixed with complementary mRNA and heated to separate the strands, then cooled so complementary sequences paired. Noncoding regions of DNA were seen as loops, revealing that some internal sequences of genes are not present in the RNA. Conclusion: coding sequences in a gene may be interrupted by noncoding sequences.

    Exons and introns

    • Exons: sequences that are present in mRNA.
    • Introns: sequences present in genes that are not present in mRNA. They are removed by RNA splicing.

    Splicing

    Splicing via the spliceosome requires two things: consensus sequences within the pre-mRNA and the spliceosome.

    Consensus sequences (Fig. 14.8)

    • 5′ splice site: at the intron-exon junction; the GU sequence in the intron is critical.
    • 3′ splice site: at the intron-exon junction; the AG sequence in the intron is critical.
    • Branch point: within the intron, an adenine (A) nucleotide 18 to 40 bp upstream of the 3′ splice site.

    Two-step splicing (Fig. 14.9)

    1. The pre-mRNA is cut at the 5′ splice site.
    2. The 5′ end of the intron attaches to the branch point, forming a lariat: the 5′ phosphate group of G attaches to the 2′ OH group of the branch-point A.
    3. A cut is made at the 3′ splice site.
    4. The intron is released as a lariat, and the two exons are spliced together.
    5. The bond holding the lariat is broken, and the linear intron is degraded.
    6. The spliced mRNA is exported to the cytoplasm and translated.

    The spliceosome

    • Composed of 5 snRNPs (small ribonuclear particles): U1, U2, U4, U5 and U6.
    • snRNP = small nuclear RNA (snRNA) + associated proteins.
    • What's special about snRNAs? They base pair complementarily with the pre-mRNA.

    snRNPs assemble stepwise (Fig. 14.10)

    1. U1 attaches to the 5′ splice site.
    2. U2 attaches to the branch point.
    3. A complex of U4, U5 and U6 joins the spliceosome.
    4. The 5′ splice site is cut and the lariat is formed.
    5. U1 and U4 are released.
    6. The 3′ splice site is cut and the 2 exons are joined covalently together.
    7. The intron is released as a lariat with U2, U5 and U6. The snRNPs dissociate and the lariat is degraded, leaving mature mRNA.
    If the sequence encoding the 5′ splice site were mutated, what would be the effect on mRNA splicing?
    The intron would be retained in the mature mRNA. (The handwritten note adds that the 5′ end, U1, would not bind to the branch point.)
    What effect would this mutation have on the protein encoded by this gene?
    A different (wrong) protein would be produced.

    Alternative Processing

    A single pre-mRNA can produce multiple mRNAs through alternative processing:

    1. Alternative splicing
    2. Multiple 3′ cleavage
    3. A single gene can undergo both alternative splicing and alternative 3′ cleavage.

    Alternative splicing (Fig. 14.12a)

    Example: a pre-mRNA has 3 exons and 2 introns. Removing the 2 introns gives an mRNA with exons 1, 2 and 3. Removing the 2 introns and exon 2 gives an mRNA with exons 1 and 3.

    Multiple 3′ cleavage (Fig. 14.12b)

    Example: exon 2 contains two possible 3′ cleavage sites. Cutting at one or the other produces mRNA products of different length.

    Tissue-specific processing of CALCA (Fig. 14.13)

    • Thyroid cells use the 3′ cleavage site in exon 4.
    • Brain cells use the 3′ cleavage site in exon 6, and exon 4 is removed during splicing.

    tRNA Processing

    tRNA structure (Fig. 14.19)

    • tRNAs are adapter molecules that connect three-letter mRNA sequences to their corresponding amino acids. In eukaryotic cells they function in the cytoplasm, helping incorporate amino acids into the polypeptide chain.
    • The anticodon is complementary to mRNA.
    • The amino acid attachment site is at the 3′ end and is always 5′-CCA-3′.
    • The structure is held by intramolecular base pairing (hydrogen bonds between paired bases).
    • It can be drawn as a space-filling model, a ribbon model or a cloverleaf model.

    Processing (Fig. 14.20)

    1. A large precursor tRNA is cleaved to produce an individual tRNA molecule.
    2. An intron is removed by splicing.
    3. Bases are added to the 3′ end.
    4. Several bases are modified, producing the mature tRNA.

    Rare bases

    • Made after transcription by tRNA-modifying enzymes.
    • They affect the structure of the tRNA and how it interacts with the ribosome and mRNA.
    • Examples made from uracil (Fig. 14.18): ribothymidine (a methyl group added) and pseudouridine.

    rRNA Processing

    Ribosomes are complex structures composed of rRNA plus many proteins. rRNA's job is to be the structural and functional components of the ribosome, in the cytoplasm. In eukaryotes, snoRNA processes and assembles rRNA.
