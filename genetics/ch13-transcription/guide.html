    RNA Structure

    RNA is a chain of nucleotides. Each nucleotide has three parts: a phosphate, a sugar and a base.

    The sugar: ribose

    RNA's sugar is ribose; DNA uses deoxyribose. The difference is at the 2′ carbon: ribose has an OH (hydroxyl) group there, while deoxyribose has just an H.

    Why it mattersThe 2′ OH is chemically more reactive, which makes RNA less stable than DNA. RNA is easily degraded; DNA is stable.

    The bases

    GroupRingsBasesRemember
    PurinesTwoAdenine, guaninePure as gold (Ag)
    PyrimidinesOneCytosine, thymine, uracilCut the pie (CUT)
    • Shared by DNA and RNA: adenine, guanine, cytosine.
    • Uracil (U) is in RNA but not DNA. Thymine (T) is in DNA but not RNA.

    Backbone and shape

    • Nucleotides are joined by phosphodiester bonds.
    • The backbone alternates phosphate groups and ribose sugars, with a 5′ to 3′ direction.
    • RNA is usually single-stranded; DNA is usually double-stranded.
    • The monomers used to build RNA are rNTPs (ribonucleoside triphosphates): A, U, G and C.

    Secondary structure

    DNA's secondary structure is a double helix. RNA can take many types of secondary structure, created by intramolecular base pairing: the strand folds back and pairs with itself. In those pairs, A pairs with U and G pairs with C.

    An RNA's base sequence determines its secondary structure, so different RNAs assume different structures. That matters because secondary structure provides function.

    Why is RNA less stable than DNA?
    Ribose's 2′ OH is chemically more reactive.

    Transcription Basics

    The central dogma: DNA is transcribed into RNA, and RNA is translated into protein. The flow of genetic information is linear, in one direction.

    Transcription vs. DNA replication

    TranscriptionDNA replication
    EnzymeRNA polymeraseDNA polymerase
    TemplateOne strand (the template strand)Both strands
    PrimerNone neededNeeds a primer
    Pairs with A in templateUracil (no thymine in RNA)
    ProductA single-stranded RNA molecule, such as mRNATwo identical dsDNA molecules
    Direction5′ to 3′5′ to 3′

    Requirements

    1. A single-stranded DNA template strand
    2. rNTPs
    3. The transcription apparatus (RNA polymerase plus other proteins)

    The two DNA strands

    • New nucleotides are always added to the 3′ end of the RNA.
    • The template strand is complementary and antiparallel to the RNA.
    • The nontemplate strand has the same sequence as the RNA (with T instead of U). It is not transcribed.
    • Either strand can serve as a gene's template. Whichever is used, the RNA is always antiparallel to it.
    Worked exampleTemplate DNA is 5′-AGGCTAG-3′. The RNA is complementary and antiparallel: 3′-UCCGAUC-5′, which read 5′ to 3′ is 5′-CUAGCCU-3′.

    Gene structure

    Upstream to downstream, a gene has three parts:

    • Promoter: DNA just upstream of the start site, where the transcription apparatus binds.
    • RNA-coding region: the template for RNA synthesis.
    • Terminator: the sequence that causes transcription to stop. It is usually part of the RNA.

    Gene structure, especially the promoter, determines where and how transcription is initiated.

    Which is NOT a promoter function: bind the apparatus, set the start, pick the strand, or signal where transcription ends?
    Signaling where transcription ends. That is the terminator's job. (Concept Check 1)

    Consensus Sequences

    A consensus sequence lists the most common nucleotides at a specific location in DNA or RNA.

    SymbolMeans
    RA purine (A or G)
    YA pyrimidine (C, T or U)
    NAny nucleotide
    C/GSplit between the two groups, so list both

    How to build one

    1. Go by majority first.
    2. If a position is split within one group, use R or Y.
    3. If it is split between the groups, list both (for example C/G).
    4. No majority and no single group: N.
    Position readsConsensusWhy
    T, C, T, CYBoth pyrimidines
    A, A, G, GRBoth purines
    A, T, C, GNNo majority and no single group
    Worked exampleSequences TATAAAAG, TCCAATGC, AATAGCCG and TACAGGAC give 5′-TAYARNA(C/G)-3′. Position by position: T, A, Y, A, R, N, A, C/G.

    Bacterial Initiation

    RNA polymerase

    • The core enzyme has five subunits: 2 α, β, β′ and ω. It synthesizes RNA.
    • The sigma (σ) factor guides the core enzyme to promoters to start transcription.
    • Holoenzyme = core enzyme + sigma factor. The holoenzyme is the form that first binds the promoter.

    Bacterial promoter

    ElementConsensusPosition
    −35 sequenceTTGACA35 bp upstream of the start site
    −10 sequenceTATAAT10 bp upstream of the start site
    Start site+1

    Initiation, step by step (Figure 13.12)

    1. Sigma joins the core to form the holoenzyme.
    2. The holoenzyme binds the −35 and −10 sequences.
    3. The holoenzyme changes conformation and unwinds the DNA, forming the transcription bubble.
    4. The first rNTP is complementary to the template at the start site. It keeps all three phosphates.
    5. For each later rNTP, two phosphates are cleaved and the nucleotide is added to the 3′ end. The remaining phosphate is in the phosphodiester bond.
    6. Sigma is released as RNA polymerase moves beyond the promoter; the core enzyme continues alone.
    What binds the −10 consensus sequence in most bacterial promoters?
    The holoenzyme (core enzyme + sigma). (Concept Check 2)

    Bacterial Elongation and Termination

    Elongation

    • The core enzyme moves downstream; sigma is already released.
    • About 18 bp are unwound in the transcription bubble.
    • Certain DNA sequences or RNA secondary structures can make RNA polymerase pause.
    • After adding a wrong nucleotide, RNA polymerase pauses, backs up and cuts the last few nucleotides, then continues.

    Bacteria terminate transcription by rho-dependent or rho-independent mechanisms.

    Rho-dependent termination

    1. Rho binds the rut site (rho utilization) on the RNA.
    2. RNA polymerase pauses downstream.
    3. Rho moves 5′ to 3′ along the RNA and catches up.
    4. Its helicase activity unwinds the RNA-DNA hybrid and releases the RNA.

    Rho-independent (intrinsic) termination

    Needs no additional protein factors. The terminator is an inverted repeat followed by about six adenine nucleotides.

    • In the RNA, the inverted repeats fold into a hairpin by intramolecular base pairing.
    • The adenines are transcribed as a string of U's. U-A base pairs are weak, so the RNA separates from the DNA.

    Eukaryotic Initiation

    Five RNA polymerases

    PolymeraseTranscribes
    Pol ILarge rRNAs
    Pol IIPre-mRNA, some snRNAs, snoRNAs and some miRNAs
    Pol IIItRNAs, small rRNAs, some snRNAs and some miRNAs
    Pol IV (plants only)Some siRNAs
    Pol V (plants only)RNAs for heterochromatin formation

    The players

    • Transcription factors: proteins that bind DNA and affect transcription levels.
    • Basal transcription apparatus: RNA polymerase plus general transcription factors.

    Pol II core promoter

    Its conserved sequences are binding sites for general transcription factors.

    ElementPosition
    BRE (TFIIB recognition element)−35
    TATA box (TATAAA)−25
    Initiator element+1
    Downstream core-promoter element (DPE)+30

    Assembly

    • TFIID binds the TATA box first (step 1 of preinitiation complex assembly).
    • TBP (TATA-binding protein) is a subunit of TFIID. It binds the minor groove and bends and partly unwinds the DNA.
    • Proteins bound to distant enhancers reach the basal apparatus because the DNA loops out.
    • Regulatory promoter transcription factors interact with the basal apparatus through the mediator.
    • After initiation, the basal transcription factors stay at the promoter and RNA Pol II moves downstream.
    • Entering DNA is gripped and unwound into the bubble; nucleotides are added to the 3′ end at Pol II's active site.
    Don't mix these upTFIID is the general transcription factor that binds the TATA box first. TBP is the subunit of TFIID that does the binding.

    PolymeraseHow it terminatesBacterial parallel

    The Rat1 mechanism (Pol II)

    1. The RNA is cleaved, generating its 3′ end. Pol II keeps transcribing past the cleavage site.
    2. Rat1, a 5′ to 3′ exonuclease, binds the 5′ end of the trailing RNA (the RNA left after the cleavage site).
    3. Rat1 degrades the RNA as it goes. Termination occurs when Rat1 reaches RNA Pol II.
    Which eukaryotic polymerase terminates most like bacterial rho-independent termination?
    RNA polymerase III, which transcribes a terminator sequence.
