    Mutation Basics

    Why mutations matter

    • Source of all genetic variation, which provides the raw material for evolution.
    • Source of many diseases and disorders.
    • Useful for probing fundamental biological processes.
    • Can be harmful, beneficial or neutral.

    Somatic vs germline

    Multicellular organisms have two broad types of tissue: germline tissue (reproductive cells that produce gametes, sperm or eggs) and somatic tissue (all other cells). The location of a mutation determines whether it is passed to offspring.

    • Somatic mutations are not inheritable. They spread by mitosis to a population of mutant cells.
    • Germline mutations are inheritable through sexual reproduction (Fig. 18.1).
    ExampleTp53, the guardian of the genome. Somatic mutations of Tp53 occur in about 50% of human cancers. Li-Fraumeni syndrome is a germline mutation of Tp53.

    Two categories

    1. Chromosomal rearrangements: large scale, affect multiple genes.
    2. Gene mutations: small changes, affect single genes.

    Chromosome Rearrangements

    Four basic types (Ch 8.2, Fig. 8.4):

    TypeWhat happens, and consequences
    DeletionPart of a chromosome is deleted (loss of genetic material). Neutral to severe defects depending on size and location.
    DuplicationPart of a chromosome is doubled (gain of genetic material). Problems in chromosome pairing during meiosis; altered gene dosage.
    InversionA segment is inverted (no loss or gain). Neutral; a gene broken in 2 parts; position effect.
    TranslocationGenetic material moves to another chromosome. Position effect; deletions frequently accompany translocations.
    • A duplication next to the original is a tandem duplication. Duplications can also be displaced and/or reversed (they may have been inserted anywhere).
    • In meiosis, a duplicated region must loop out to let the homologous sequences align (Fig. 8.6).
    • Gene duplication and subsequent divergent evolution are primary drivers of biological complexity and new traits. Flies have p53; humans have p53, p63 and p73.

    Gene Mutations

    Three basic types (Ch 18.1): base pair substitutions, insertions/deletions (indels) and expanding nucleotide repeats.

    Base pair substitutions

    Alteration of a single nucleotide. They may or may not alter the encoded amino acid.

    Slide checkSlide 18 prints "Ser" under the GCA codon, but GCA codes for Ala in the codon table, as the handwritten note on slide 17 says.
    • Transition: purine for purine, or pyrimidine for pyrimidine (s = same). A↔G, T↔C.
    • Transversion: pyrimidine for purine, or purine for pyrimidine (v = versa). Usually more severe.

    Indels

    Addition or removal of one or more nucleotides. Consequence: a frameshift mutation, which alters the encoded amino acids at or following the mutation. Notice how the codons shift over.

    Expanding nucleotide repeats

    • Genes contain short tandem repeats (STRs), found anywhere in the gene: promoter, 5′UTR, exon, intron or 3′UTR.
    • STRs are expanded in the disease state, which can affect the protein produced and/or how the gene is regulated.
    • The number of copies of a set of nucleotides increases. Most are expansions of a 3-nucleotide set (CNG). More copies means more severe disease.
    • ALS is a human genetic disease caused by expanding nucleotide repeats (Lou Gehrig, diagnosed at 36). Table 18.1 lists others; the slide says you do not need to memorize it.

    A model for repeat expansion (Fig. 18.5)

    1. Occurs during DNA replication.
    2. A hairpin forms on the newly synthesized strand (DNA polymerase stutters, then reattaches).
    3. Part of the template is replicated twice, increasing the number of repeats.
    4. The DNA with increased repeats is used as a template, so the result contains extra CAG copies (8 → 13 in the figure).

    Spontaneous Mutations

    Mutations are classified by cause: spontaneous (mistakes in replication or meiosis, or spontaneous chemical changes in DNA) or induced (environmental mutagens, chemical or radiation). Spontaneous mutation rates are rare and vary among organisms and genes (Table 18.3).

    Mistakes in replication or meiosis

    • Wobble: flexibility of DNA strands permits mispairing, such as thymine-guanine and cytosine-adenine (adenine protonated, with an extra hydrogen). It takes two rounds of replication to make the error permanent, and it leads to base pair substitutions.
    • Strand slippage: a loop in the newly synthesized strand adds a nucleotide (insertion); a loop in the template omits one (deletion).
    • Unequal crossing over: in meiosis I (prophase I), homologous chromosomes may misalign. One product has an insertion, the other a deletion.
    • Expanding nucleotide repeats (previous section).
    Not the sameWobble in Ch 18 is mispairing of DNA bases. Wobble in Ch 15 is flexible codon-anticodon pairing at the 3rd codon base, behind the degeneracy of the code.

    Spontaneous chemical changes

    • Depurination: a base, usually a purine (A or G, "Pure As Gold"), is lost when the covalent bond between base and sugar breaks. Pyrimidines are C, U or T ("CUT the py"). The apurinic site cannot template, so a roughly random base (often A) is incorporated; at the next round it becomes a permanent mutation (Fig. 18.14).
    • Oxidative deamination: loss of an amino group from a base, changing it into one that pairs differently.
      • Cytosine → uracil: C•G → U•A → T•A.
      • 5-methylcytosine (a naturally occurring modified base) → thymine: C•G → 5mC•G → T•G → T•A.
      • Both are C to T base pair substitutions, so they are transitions.
    • Oxidation by reactive oxygen: produced by normal metabolism, elevated by chemical mutagens and ionizing radiation. Guanine becomes 8-oxy-7,8-dihydrodeoxyguanine, which may mispair with adenine, causing a G-C to T-A transversion (G•C → 8-oxoG•C → 8-oxoG•A → T•A).

    Induced Mutations

    Base analogs

    Molecules that mimic the structure of normal DNA bases. 5-bromouracil (5BU) has a Br atom instead of a methyl group on carbon 5. Like T, it normally pairs with A, but when ionized it pairs with G (wobble). Result: a permanent A-T to G-C transition after two additional rounds of replication (Fig. 18.18).

    Chemical mutagens cause base pair substitutions

    MutagenChangeMutation
    Alkylating agents (EMS)Guanine → O6-ethylguanine, pairs with TCG → TA, TA → CG
    Oxidative deamination (nitrous acid)Cytosine → uracil, pairs with ACG → TA, TA → CG
    HydroxylamineCytosine → hydroxylaminocytosine, pairs with ACG → TA

    Intercalating agents

    Flat molecules (e.g. acridine orange) that intercalate between bases. They distort the double helix, increase strand slippage and cause single-nucleotide insertions and deletions during replication.

    Establishing the triplet codeCrick and colleagues used acridine orange to make mutants with single-nucleotide insertions or deletions. Adding or removing a base shifts the entire reading frame downstream; three additions restore the reading frame, which established that the code is a triplet.

    Radiation

    • Ionizing radiation (X-rays, gamma rays) breaks chemical bonds in water and other molecules, which oxidize bases or break the DNA backbone.
    • Ultraviolet light (about 260 nm, absorbed by bases) causes pyrimidine dimers, which block replication.

    Phenotypic Effects

    • Forward mutation: wild-type phenotype → mutant phenotype.
    • Reverse mutation: mutant phenotype → wild-type phenotype.

    Ames test (Fig. 18.23)

    Tests whether chemicals are mutagens and therefore may cause cancer, by testing for reversion of his− auxotrophic bacteria. Colonies that grow underwent a his− to his+ mutation. Any chemical that significantly increases the number of colonies on the treatment plate is mutagenic and probably carcinogenic.

    Conditional mutation: the phenotype is only expressed under certain conditions. Siamese cats carry a recessive temperature-sensitive allele of an albinism gene; the mutant protein is only active in cooler cells in the extremities.

    Suppressor mutations

    A second mutation that masks the phenotype of another without reverting it.

    • Intragenic: in the same gene. It may change the codon so the wild-type amino acid is encoded (Fig. 18.8: UUA Leu → UUU Phe → CUU Leu), or change a different amino acid to compensate.
    • Intergenic: in a different gene. In Fig. 18.9, a nonsense mutation makes UAG; a second mutation in a tyrosine tRNA gene changes its anticodon from AUA to AUC, which pairs with UAG (not normally possible). Tyr is inserted and a full-length, functional protein is made.

    Mismatch repair

    The mismatch repair complex detects the mismatch and interacts with protein bound to hemi-methylated GATC. Right after replication the old strand is methylated and the new one is not yet, which identifies the new strand. Single-strand nicks are made next to the unmethylated GATC; exonuclease removes the strand, DNA polymerase replaces it, and DNA ligase seals the nick.

    Direct repair

    Does not replace the altered nucleotide; it restores the correct structure. Example: methyltransferase removes the methyl group from O6-methylguanine, restoring guanine.

    Base excision repair

    1. A specific DNA glycosylase recognizes and removes the modified base (e.g. uracil glycosylase removes uracil), creating an AP (apurinic or apyrimidinic) site.
    2. AP endonuclease cleaves the phosphodiester bond next to the AP site.
    3. DNA polymerase recognizes the nick and replaces the missing nucleotide.
    4. DNA ligase seals the nick.

    Nucleotide excision repair

    Recognizes and removes bulky distortions in the double helix, such as pyrimidine dimers. An enzyme complex recognizes the distortion and recruits enzymes that separate the strands; single-strand binding proteins keep them apart. The damaged strand is nicked on both sides, helicases remove it, DNA polymerase fills the gap and DNA ligase seals the nick.

    Double-strand breaks

    • Homologous recombination: sequence from the sister chromatid replaces the broken region (the preferred method). Uses BRCA1 and BRCA2.
    • Nonhomologous end joining: proteins bind the broken ends and join them. Very error prone, but may let the cell survive. It can form deletions, duplications, inversions and translocations.
    Disease linkInheriting a mutant allele of a DNA repair gene raises the somatic mutation rate. If BRCA1 or BRCA2 is mutated, the cell must use nonhomologous end joining (higher mutation chance), which greatly increases risk for breast and ovarian cancer.

    Transposable Elements

    Transposable elements ("jumping genes") are DNA sequences that can move from one genomic location to another. Transposition can be mutagenic and a source of genomic instability. They make up 46% of the human genome; protein-coding sequence is 3%.

    Two classes (Table 18.4)

    Class I retrotransposonsClass II DNA transposons
    MechanismCopy and paste via an RNA intermediateCut and paste via DNA
    GenesReverse transcriptase and endonucleaseTransposase
    EndsLong terminal direct repeatsShort terminal inverted repeats
    ExamplesTy (yeast), copia (Drosophila), Alu (human)IS1, Tn3 (E. coli), Ac and Ds (corn), P elements (Drosophila)
    • Transposase binds the inverted repeats and cuts the DNA; the DNA transposon inserts at a new site.
    • Retrotransposon endonuclease nicks or cleaves the DNA so the copy can integrate.

    Non-autonomous elements

    The Ac element has an intact transposase gene. Ds elements have truncated, nonfunctional transposase genes (different Ds elements have different deletions) and "hijack" the Ac transposase to jump.

    Effects

    • Some bacterial transposons carry antibiotic resistance genes; Tn10 carries tetR between IS10L and IS10R. Their transposition can make multi-drug resistant DNA that passes between bacteria.
    • Elements may insert into or next to genes and disrupt expression. White grapes: a Gret1 retrotransposon near VvmybA1 disrupts anthocyanin synthesis. Red grapes: most of it was removed, partly restoring color.
    • LINE-1 is an autonomous human retrotransposon with about 150 full-length copies. Its retrotransposition is a source of DNA damage and genome instability, and its proteins are biomarkers for cancer.
