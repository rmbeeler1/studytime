Proteins

Amino acids

20 common amino acids are found in proteins. All share a common structure around the α carbon (an amino group, a carboxyl group, a hydrogen) but differ at the R (radical) group, or side chain. The R group is unique to each amino acid and determines its chemical properties. Notice the polarity: the amino group is positive (+H3N), the carboxyl group negative (COO−).

Peptide bonds and polypeptides

Amino acids are joined by peptide bonds: the carboxyl group of one amino acid is covalently bound to the amino group of another, releasing water (Fig. 15.6).

A polypeptide is a long, continuous chain of amino acids linked by peptide bonds (10 to 50+ amino acids). It has polarity:

  • N-terminus: free amino group (+ charge).
  • C-terminus: free carboxyl group (− charge).

Protein structure (Fig. 15.7)

The amino acid sequence determines the 3D structure and function of the protein, because different R groups have different properties.

  • Primary: the sequence of amino acids.
  • Secondary: interactions between amino acids fold the primary structure, for example into an alpha helix.
  • Tertiary: the secondary structure folds further.
  • Quaternary: two or more polypeptide chains associate.

Functions

  • Enzymes: biological catalysts that drive the chemical reactions of a cell.
  • Structural: provide shape, rigidity and strength to cells, tissues and organs.
  • And many more: defense and immunity, transport and storage, signaling and regulation.

One Gene One Enzyme

The one gene, one enzyme hypothesis proposed that each gene in an organism encodes a single enzyme (protein). Beadle and Tatum shared the 1958 Nobel Prize for it.

Neurospora and auxotrophs

  • Neurospora is a filamentous fungus. It can grow in rich media (all amino acids supplied) or in minimal media with no amino acids, where it synthesizes all required amino acids and other essential biomolecules using enzymes.
  • Auxotrophic: unable to produce a particular nutrient.
  • Key to success: the main vegetative part of the fungus is haploid (one copy of each chromosome).
  • Spores were mutated with X-rays, grown on complete medium, then tested on minimal medium. A mutant that grows on minimal medium supplemented with arginine must be defective for arginine production.

Arginine synthesis

Arginine is produced stepwise from a precursor: precursor → ornithine → citrulline → arginine. An auxotrophic mutant cannot grow on a compound that comes before the blocked step, but can grow on anything after it.

Srb and Horowitz recovered several arginine auxotrophs (Table 15.1; + means growth, − no growth):

Group I blocks a step before ornithine, Group II a step before citrulline, and Group III a step before arginine.

ConclusionEach gene encodes a separate protein, in this case an enzyme: gene A, B and C each make the enzyme for one step. One gene codes for one enzyme!

Concept Check

Auxotrophic mutation 103 grows on minimal medium supplemented with A, B or C. Mutation 106 grows on A and C, but not B. Mutation 102 grows only on C. What is the order of A, B and C?

Worked exampleTreat it like Table 15.1: 103 grows on everything, so it is Group I (earliest block); 106 is Group II; 102 is Group III. C must be last, since it rescues all three. B rescues only 103, so it comes first. Order: precursor → B → A → C, made by enzymes 103, 106 and 102.

The Genetic Code

DNA and RNA are made of nucleotides; proteins are made of amino acids. A codon is the set of nucleotides that encodes a single amino acid. There are 4 nucleotides but 20 amino acids:

Codon lengthCombinations
1 nucleotide1 × 4 = 4
2 nucleotides4 × 4 = 16
3 nucleotides4 × 4 × 4 = 64

The genetic code is a triplet code!

Cracking it: two methods

  1. Homopolymers (Nirenberg and Matthaei, Fig. 15.8). In vitro translation of RNA homopolymers such as poly(U), 5′-UUUUUUUUUUUU-3′, made with polynucleotide phosphorylase. Each of 20 tubes had a different radioactively labeled amino acid; they saw which one was incorporated into polypeptide. UUU encodes phenylalanine; in other experiments AAA encoded lysine and CCC encoded proline.
  2. Ribosome-bound tRNAs (Nirenberg and Leder, Fig. 15.9). Add a 3-nucleotide RNA of known sequence to ribosomes, add tRNAs carrying a specific radioactively labeled amino acid, and find which aminoacyl-tRNA binds the ribosome + RNA. The mixture is passed through a filter: tRNAs paired with ribosome-bound mRNA stick, unbound tRNAs pass. With GUU, the bound tRNAs carried valine, so GUU specifies valine. The anticodon of the tRNA base pairs complementarily with the mRNA.

Degeneracy and Wobble

Degeneracy: multiple three-letter codons can encode the same single amino acid.

  • Isoaccepting tRNAs: tRNAs with different anticodons attached to the same amino acid. For example, GUU, GUC, GUA and GUG all encode Val.
  • Wobble: flexibility of base pairing between the 3rd base of the codon and a base in the anticodon allows some tRNAs to base pair with more than one codon. Pairing at the third codon position is relaxed: it does not have to be perfectly matched.
  • Example (Fig. 15.11): a G in the anticodon can pair with C or with U. The tRNA with anticodon 3′-AGG-5′ reads both UCC and UCU, which both encode Ser.

In Fig. 15.14, tRNA positions in blue are the same in all tRNAs and cannot be used to differentiate them; positions in red are important in recognition by one synthetase; positions in yellow are used by more than one synthetase.

Non-overlapping

The code is a series of consecutive, distinct, 3-base codons. The AUG start codon determines the reading frame. AUACGAGUC reads AUA CGA GUC = Ile, Arg, Val. An overlapping code would instead read AUA, UAC, ACG = Ile, Tyr, Thr.

Translation Basics

Translation is the process by which a protein is assembled from the information contained in an mRNA. The mRNA is read 5′ to 3′ and the polypeptide grows from its N-terminus to its C-terminus (Fig. 15.12).

Tools: mRNA, "charged" tRNAs and the ribosome. Steps: initiation, elongation, termination.

tRNAs are the code-breakers

  • Amino acids are attached to the 3′ ends of tRNAs.
  • tRNAs are "adapter" molecules connecting the language of nucleic acids to the language of proteins.
  • Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase attaches a specific amino acid to its corresponding tRNA. ATP provides the energy.
  • A tRNA attached to its amino acid is charged.

Ribosomes

Ribosomes are the sites of polypeptide synthesis: rRNA plus many proteins, in a small and a large subunit. Bacterial ribosomes are 70S (50S + 30S); eukaryotic ribosomes are 80S (60S + 40S) (Table 14.3).

Initiation

Bacteria: find the AUG

The Shine-Dalgarno sequence, a consensus sequence in the 5′ untranslated region of the mRNA, base pairs with part of the 16S rRNA. This positions the initiation codon at the correct place on the small subunit.

  1. The ribosome consists of two subunits (50S and 30S).
  2. IF-3 binds the small subunit, preventing the large subunit from binding,
  3. which allows the small subunit to attach to the mRNA.
  4. A tRNA charged with N-formylmethionine forms a complex with IF-2 and GTP,
  5. which binds the initiation codon while IF-1 joins the small subunit: the 30S initiation complex.
  6. All initiation factors dissociate, and GTP is hydrolyzed to GDP.
  7. The large subunit joins to create the 70S initiation complex.
ConclusionAt the end of initiation, the ribosome is assembled on the mRNA and the first tRNA is attached to the initiation codon.

Eukaryotes

  • Cap-binding proteins bind the 5′ cap; proteins attached to the 3′ poly(A) tail interact with them and enhance binding of the ribosome to the 5′ end of the mRNA (they recruit the ribosome).
  • Only the small subunit binds at first.
  • The initiation complex scans along the mRNA 5′ to 3′ until it reaches the initiation codon.
  • The Kozak sequence, 5′-ACCAUGG-3′, surrounds the start codon and is detected by the small subunit.

The Ch 15 version of Table 14.2 adds: the 5′ cap also enhances RNA splicing; the poly(A) tail also aids export of mRNA from the nucleus; and internal RNA modifications influence splicing, mRNA degradation and translation.

Elongation

tRNAs may occupy three sites in a ribosome: the aminoacyl (A) site for incoming codons, the peptidyl (P) site and the exit (E) site. At the start, fMet-tRNA occupies the P site of the 70S initiation complex.

  1. EF-Tu-GTP brings a charged tRNA to the A site (EF-Tu, GTP and the charged tRNA form a complex).
  2. After the tRNA is placed in the A site, GTP is cleaved to GDP and EF-Tu-GDP is released. EF-Ts regenerates EF-Tu-GTP.
  3. rRNA in the large ribosomal subunit catalyzes the peptide bond between the amino acid in the A site and the amino acid on the tRNA in the P site. The P-site tRNA releases its amino acid; the dipeptide is now attached to the tRNA in the A site.
  4. Translocation: hydrolysis of GTP bound to EF-G moves the ribosome 3 nucleotides along the mRNA, 5′ to 3′. The tRNA without an amino acid moves to the E site and exits to the cytoplasm, the peptidyl-tRNA is in the P site, and the next codon is in the A site.
ConclusionAt the end of each cycle, the amino acid that was in the A site has been added to the chain and the A site is free for another tRNA. The steps repeat along the length of the mRNA. Reading frame: set by the AUG start codon, read in triplets, non-overlapping.
In elongation, the creation of peptide bonds between amino acids is catalyzed by: rRNA, protein in the small subunit, protein in the large subunit, or tRNA?
rRNA, in the large subunit. (Concept Check)

In the 3D ribosome, the growing polypeptide leaves through the ribosomal exit tunnel in the large (50S) subunit.

Termination

A stop codon enters the A site. It is not recognized by a charged tRNA (no tRNA has a matching anticodon), but it is recognized by a release factor (RF).

  1. RF-1 attaches to the A site,
  2. and RF-3 forms a complex with GTP and binds the ribosome.
  3. The polypeptide is released from the tRNA in the P site.
  4. The GTP associated with RF-3 is hydrolyzed to GDP.
  5. The tRNA, mRNA and release factors are released from the ribosome.
SummarytRNA charging, initiation, elongation, termination and peptide release generate a polypeptide chain specified by the mRNA codons (Fig. 15.19).

Polyribosomes

A polyribosome is an mRNA with multiple ribosomes, so the cell can make lots of protein all the time. In the bacterial image, ribosomes are already translating an RNA that is still being transcribed from the DNA.
