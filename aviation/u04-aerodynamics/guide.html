    Forces

    Four forces act on an airplane in flight: lift, weight, thrust and drag.

    Vectors

    A vector is a quantity with direction and magnitude. Vectors are drawn as arrows: the arrow points the direction, and its length shows the magnitude.

    Lift

    • The wings create lift, and lift overcomes weight.
    • Lift acts perpendicular to the relative wind. Relative wind is the airflow the wing meets; lift points at 90° to it.

    Balanced flight

    In straight-and-level flight at constant airspeed, lift equals weight and thrust equals drag. When all forces are balanced, neither airspeed nor altitude changes.

    Think in componentsIn steady flight it is not just lift versus weight. Every force has components: the sum of all upward components equals the sum of all downward components, and the sum of all forward components equals the sum of all backward components.

    Newton's laws

    • First law (inertia): an object keeps its motion unless an unbalanced force acts on it. Balanced flight illustrates this.
    • Third law: for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. Examples: a rocket launch, or a propeller's torque reaction.
    Balanced flight, with no change in speed or altitude, illustrates which of Newton's laws?
    Newton's first law (inertia).

    Lift Theory

    An airfoil's front is the leading edge and its back is the trailing edge.

    The slides say NOThe longer path (equal transit time) theory is false. It claims air over the top of the wing must travel faster to meet the lower air at the trailing edge, and uses that to explain low pressure on top.

    Its false assumption: that air splitting at the leading edge must rejoin. Molecules arriving at the leading edge at the same time do not have to reach the trailing edge at the same time.

    Airfoils

    Many surfaces on an airplane act as airfoils and create aerodynamic forces that help maneuver it.

    Lift Factors

    Four factors set how much lift a wing makes. A pilot controls three of them.

    FactorPilot controls it byEffect on lift
    Angle of attackPitching up or downHigher AOA forces more airflow downward: more lift
    AirspeedAdjusting power, or climbing or descendingExponential: double the velocity, quadruple the lift
    CamberExtending or retracting flaps (the only way)More curve: more lift
    Wing surface areaGenerally not controllableLinear: double the area, double the lift

    Angle of attack

    Angle of attack (α, alpha, or AOA) is the angle between the chord line and the relative wind.

    Airspeed

    • Airspeed is measured relative to the air the aircraft is moving through, not the ground.
    • Higher airspeed means the airfoil contacts more air molecules, so it makes more lift.
    • Aircraft usually make their own relative wind by creating thrust. Not always: a strong wind alone can create enough lift to make an aircraft fly.
    • A change in airspeed changes the velocity term in the lift equation. Its effect is exponential, not linear.

    Camber

    Camber is an airfoil's curvature. Flaps are lift-enhancing devices: flaps down = more camber. Pilots change the camber of the horizontal and vertical stabilizers by moving the elevator and rudder; deflecting a control surface curves the stabilizer's airfoil and changes its lift.

    Wing surface area

    The area of the wing exposed to airflow. Its effect is linear.

    Doubling an aircraft's velocity does what to lift?
    Quadruples it (4×).
    Which lift factor can a pilot generally NOT control?
    Wing surface area.

    Stalls

    As angle of attack increases, the coefficient of lift increases until the critical angle of attack. Beyond it the wing stalls: airflow separates from the top of the wing and lift drops.

    • For a given airplane and configuration (such as flap setting), a stall always occurs at the same angle of attack, set by the aircraft's design. It can still happen at different airspeeds.
    • Angle of attack is a variable the pilot can control, by pitching up or down.

    Normal vs. accelerated stalls

    Normal stallAccelerated stall
    Load factor1G (what you feel sitting in your chair)More than 1G
    AirspeedHigher than a normal stall
    ExampleA steep turn or a quick pull-up

    Designing for safer stalls

    Designers want stalls to:

    1. Keep the aircraft controllable as long as possible
    2. Be delayed as long as possible
    3. Give pilots warning

    Predictable stall characteristics make aircraft safer and easier to control. Stall mitigation tools: wing washout, camber, vortex generators and planform selection.

    Washout

    The wing is twisted so the tip has a lower AOA than the root. The root (inboard section) stalls first and the tip stalls last, which keeps the ailerons effective and warns the pilot the whole wing is about to stall.

    Weight and Balance

    Center of lift

    The center of lift, also called the center of pressure, is where lift forces are concentrated. For balance it sits slightly aft (behind) the CG.

    Arms, moments and the datum

    • The datum (reference datum) is the point all arm measurements are taken from. The manufacturer chooses it; it is an arbitrary point.
    • An arm is measured in inches from the datum.
    • Moment = arm × weight.
    • In a balanced aircraft, positive plus negative moments at the CG equal zero.
    • CG = total moment ÷ total weight

    Four steps to find CG

    1. Find each weight's arm in inches from the datum.
    2. Multiply arm by weight to get each moment.
    3. Total all weights and all moments.
    4. Divide total moment by total weight.
    Worked exampleWeights of 100 at +50, 100 at +90 and 200 at +150 inches.
    Moments: 5,000 + 9,000 + 30,000 = 44,000. Total weight: 400.
    CG = 44,000 ÷ 400 = +110 inches from the datum.

    CG out of limits

    CG too farDanger
    ForwardNot enough elevator nose-up force to flare for landing
    AftNot enough nose-down force for stall recovery, especially at low airspeed
    • An airplane can be within its allowable weight limit but outside its CG limit. Pilots may discover this during preflight.
    • Correct an out-of-limit CG by shifting or removing weight.
    • Weight changes in flight (fuel burn, skydiving operations, passengers moving themselves or luggage) can shift the CG.

    Propellers

    Torque reaction

    A clockwise-spinning propeller causes a counterclockwise reaction from the airplane, giving it a left-turning tendency. This is Newton's third law: engine torque is the action; the left gear forced downward is the reaction.

    Torque effect isWhenWhy
    StrongestLow speed with high power, like takeoff
    WeakerHigher airspeedAirflow over control surfaces counters it
    WeakerLower power settingThe propeller spins slower

    Variable-pitch propellers

    Blade angle is the propeller's pitch. Changing it in flight keeps the most efficient AOA at any speed. The most common type is the constant-speed propeller. It adjusts its blades with oil pressure from the engine, and the control is a knob or lever next to the throttle.

    PhasePitchRPM
    TakeoffLowHigh
    CruiseHighLow

    Drag

    Parasite drag

    Three types: form, interference and skin friction. Parasite drag varies with the square of the airspeed.

    Worked exampleParasite drag at 160 knots is 4 times that at 80 knots: double the airspeed, square the effect, 2² = 4.

    Skin friction drag

    Created by air resistance moving across the airplane's surface: the skin catches air particles and tries to pull the air along. Even a smooth surface makes some, because it is rough at the microscopic level. To reduce it, use smooth skin (especially composite aircraft) and flush rivets. Both improve performance and fuel efficiency.

    L/D ratio

    L/D = CL ÷ CD: lift produced divided by drag created. The lift and drag equations differ only by this coefficient, so the ratio uses just that term. A higher L/D means the aircraft produces lift more efficiently.

    Ground effect

    The ground interferes with the wingtip vortices, which cannot create as much downwash. Less downwash means less induced drag, so ground effect is really a reduction of induced drag. It is most pronounced within half a wingspan of the ground and feels like a cushion of air.

    Ground effect is actually a reduction of which type of drag?
    Induced drag.
